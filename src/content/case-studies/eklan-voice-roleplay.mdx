decisions:
  - choice: streaming straight from the browser to Gemini Live with locked, short-lived tokens
    over: relaying every audio chunk through our own server
    because: the relay added a hop to every turn and a server to keep alive, while a locked token keeps the model, instructions and voice under server control without carrying the audio
  - choice: the app owns the scene clock
    over: letting the model decide when a phase is over
    because: in testing the model asked to move on far too early, so its phase-change calls became requests the app accepts only near the end of a phase
  - choice: one live session per character, plus a fast director model
    over: one session that voices everyone
    because: a single session drifts between voices and personas; separate sessions keep each character's voice and role stable, and the director only has to answer one small question per turn
challenges:
  - problem: Some characters spoke in the wrong voice, and nothing in the logs said why.
    solution: The Live API silently accepted voice names it didn't know and fell back to a default voice. I wrote a check that rendered all 30 candidate voices and compared their pitch with the character each was meant for, found 8 that didn't read consistently, and limited every cast to voices that passed three runs out of three.
  - problem: In loud wards the model kept waiting for silence that never came.
    solution: I tuned voice-activity detection, added a noise gate in the audio worklet, and gave learners a hold-to-talk mode and an "I'm done" control for the noisiest rooms.
  - problem: The model sometimes spoke when it wasn't its turn, or answered as another character.
    solution: The floor guard holds back unprompted turns, and the speaker guard cuts the audio and re-asks the right character when the voice and the speaker don't match.

## The problem

Eklan trains nurses and clinicians, many of them Korean-speaking, to communicate in English before they start work in English-speaking hospitals. Reading a dialogue is easy; answering a worried relative out loud, in real time, is the part that needs practice. The first version of the voice roleplay sent every turn through a server relay. It was slow, every scene had one voice, and the model decided for itself when a scene moved on.

## Who it was for

Learners practise a scene that a tutor prepared for their class: a patient with a chart, a relative with questions, a doctor giving instructions. Tutors author and publish the scenes; learners join from the browser and talk.

## My role

I designed and built the voice system end to end as the founding AI engineer: the token-locked streaming, the audio engine, voice-activity tuning, phase pacing, the multi-character director, the guards and the test harness. The screens themselves were designed by a teammate, and I merged that work onto this engine.

## What I built

<FeatureGrid items={frontmatter.features} />

In an ensemble scene, characters also speak up on their own at set points in a phase, but only in natural gaps in the conversation. Cue to first audio had a median of about 1.9 s, with no two voices overlapping in the final checks, measured in testing against the live models.

## How it works

The learner's browser captures audio and streams it straight to Gemini Live, using a short-lived token that the server has locked. When a scene has several characters, a fast director model picks who answers each turn. Guards decide what is allowed to play, and the app, not the model, keeps the scene's clock.

A voice agent can't be checked by reading its code, so I also built an automated harness that uses the same session setup and the same audio engine as the browser. A synthetic student speaks with text-to-speech audio, 19 checks cover voice, turn-taking, phase timing, response time and overlap, and an LLM judge reads the transcript. Every prompt or engine change was measured with it before release.

## What I learned

> With a live voice model, the model is one component. Timing, turn-taking and who gets to speak belong to the application.

The biggest gains came from taking decisions away from the model: who keeps time, who may speak, which voice belongs to which character. The other lesson was about rolling back. Two reliability commits for the multi-character mode made it less stable in testing, so I went back to the last stable version the same day and kept the commits recoverable instead of patching forward.
